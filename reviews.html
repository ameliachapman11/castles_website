Comments and reviews

Here are some reviews and comments from a variety of readers.
🕮 Castles in the Air is a mind-blowing fantasy about some of the deepest questions in the philosophy of mathematics. With gripping prose, Longley tells a story that will be accessible to interested outsiders, but stimulating to the cognoscenti. The extended chapter commentaries, available on the book’s website, contains one of the best surveys of the issues that I’ve seen. This genre-bending adventure should be of interest to mathematicians, philosophers, computer scientists, and everyone else who is rightly puzzled by the mystery of mathematics.

Justin Clarke-Doane, Department of Philosophy, Columbia University, author of Mathematics and Metaphilosophy.
🕮 I am neither a mathematician nor a philosopher, but this book gave me much insight about why so many people choose to be those things. Longley has achieved the remarkable feat of making some of the deepest debates in mathematics and logic accessible to non-specialist readers. We see these questions through the eyes of two young sisters as they explore the contents of first one castle, then many more, in their quest to understand the nature of mathematical truths. The writing is playful, with clever puns and a host of characters who come to the sisters either to guide them or in some cases to try to intimidate them. The writing also lets readers work to understand questions for themselves before reading on, something I really valued.

This is not maths in the way we many have met it at school, but something far, far deeper and more significant. Longley has written a superb guide to it, accessible to anybody who is willing to be taken out of their comfort zone and be asked to think.

I know I will re-read this book many times. It has joined a set of ‘desert island’ books - those few I would choose to have with me if I were allowed no more. Another of those is Douglas Hofstadter’s ‘Gödel, Escher, Bach’: if you loved that book, you will love this one too.

Prof. Jamie Davies, University of Edinburgh, author of Life Unfolding and 8 other books.
🕮 Castles in the Air tells an engrossing adventure story, in which we follow the explorations of Anna and Emily around shadowy castles, expansive landscapes and airy, magical worlds, through which they acquaint themselves with the wonders of mathematics and the principles on which it is founded. Seamlessly interwoven into their adventures is a tale of mathematics itself and the mathematicians that created it. The adventurers encounter Alan Turing and his halting problem, Kurt Gödel and his incompleteness theorems, as well as a multitude of other intriguing characters and their fascinating contributions.

Remarkably, the mathematical content is embedded within the context of a thoroughly entertaining story in such a way that the book can be enjoyably read at many levels, depending on the extent to which the reader wishes to invest in the mathematics itself. For those with the interest to put the effort in, Castles in the Air explains serious mathematical results (such as Gödel’s incompleteness theorems) in an accessible way without cutting corners. One gets the genuine article, not a popular approximation.

The book also provides the most cogent exposition of the philosophical repercussions of Gödel’s groundbreaking discoveries that I have read. One particularly significant consequence is that mathematics is less rooted in objective certainty than is commonly supposed, meaning that mathematical knowledge is necessarily dependent on a degree of mathematical belief.

The two adventurers differ fundamentally in their own beliefs, leading them on diverging paths of exploration as they struggle to come to terms with their personal disagreements. Their eventual reconciliation provides the book with the ending it deserves, as the reader is left gazing with them into a starry sky with a profound sense of wonder at nature, at human relationships and at the timeless beauty of mathematics.

Prof. Alex Simpson, Faculty of Mathematics and Physics, University of Ljubljana
🕮 As a computer science undergraduate, my interest in math had been fading away as I was failing to see its relevance for my field. And then I read Castles in the Air and decided to pursue a career in mathematical foundations of computer science. This book showed me the essence of proof and what makes it beautiful, through visual metaphors that maintain mathematical precision. I encountered wild concepts such as large cardinals, models for logic and axiomatic set theory, and discovered that mathematics does not happen in impenetrable textbooks, but rather in the minds of curious people - like me. Moreover, I found that despite the rigour of the subject, there are many underlying assumptions that I may accept or reject, leading to different systems of thought. Mathematics is alive and arises from the choices of its community, which the book motivated me to join. I would only add that since the topics covered present deep theoretical results, the middle chapters provide some heavy reading. However, each sentence was approachable and helpful, and with each chapter I felt like new universes had opened before me. I believe that anyone with an appreciation for the beauty of mathematics will enjoy this book as much as I did.

Codrin Iftode, Edinburgh
🕮 I was introduced to Castles in the Air by a recommendation on mathstodon.xyz. It’s wonderful. The narrative structure follows a couple of brilliant sisters’ journey to understand what math/proof is, from a few examples in number theory, to formal axiomatic systems, model theory, incompleteness, large cardinals, then back down to doubt over everything including finite natural numbers. Guest appearances are made by a Who’s Who including Gödel, Tarski, Russell, Cohen, Kleene, …

This is great, plus there are extensive commentaries, with references to the literature, with even more extensive commentaries on the book’s web site.

If these topics interest you, this charming book might too.
